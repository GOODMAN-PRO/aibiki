# AIBIKI — SERIES BIBLE

アイビキ / AIBIKI — *Double Act*

Expands `bible/pillars.md`. Contradicts nothing in it. Speaker names, faces and voices are
governed by `bible/cast.md`; where this file names a person, it defers to that file.
Standalone property: nothing here is shared with, borrowed from, or aware of any other series.

## 1. TITLE BLOCK

**AIBIKI** · **アイビキ** · *Double Act*

Seven years ago, on partner-picking day at a comedy school, two students were still standing
when the room finished pairing off, and formed the act nobody wanted. Today AIBIKI is the best
unknown manzai duo in the city: HAYASE KOMAKO, a once-a-decade stage animal who cannot write a
single word, and TOKITA GAKU, a timing savant who counts laughs in quarter-seconds and cannot
carry a line alone. They share one microphone under a rail embankment, one bank balance, one
forty-minute walk home, and a name they took off a family-restaurant menu — the ¥690
mixed-mince hamburg steak, two cheap meats, better ground together. Written another way, that
same word is the old term for a lovers' secret meeting. They both noticed. Seven years ago.
Neither has ever said it, because the act is both their livelihoods and the only thing either
has ever been good at, and because the two old people who run their agency and their home
theater were once the greatest duo in the country, married each other, killed the act in
fourteen months, and have not spoken in twenty-three years — and our two commute between the
halves of that wreck every day of their lives. There is a national tournament with a
¥10,000,000 prize, open only to acts inside their first ten years. AIBIKI is in year eight.
Everybody asks. They always answer the same way, in unison, flat: "We're colleagues."

**TAGLINE:** On stage they finish each other's sentences. Off stage they can't start one.

**LENGTH:** 55 chapters. One complete story. Chapter 55 lands.

## 2. TONE MANIFESTO — ten rules for the writers' room

These are not moods. Each rule carries a test. If a page fails the test, the page is wrong.

**1. PERFORM IT, DON'T REPORT IT.**
A bit that happens in a caption did not happen. Comedy is executed on the page — in bubbles, in
panel rhythm, with the audience drawn. *Test:* delete every caption box. Would the reader still
laugh? If the honest answer is "they'd know it was meant to be funny," rewrite the scene.

**2. THE KNIFE COMES AFTER THE LAUGH, NEVER INSTEAD OF IT.**
No chapter buys its ache by skipping the joke. Land the bit at full strength, let the room go,
then put the blade in while the reader is still open. *Test:* mark the funniest panel. The
saddest panel must land within three panels of it, and never before it.

**3. FEELINGS LIVE IN OBJECTS, CHORES, AND TIMING.**
Nobody here explains how they feel, because nobody here is a person who would. A hot coffee
handed over that he never drinks. ¥1,000 into an envelope. One more dryer cycle. A
quarter-second of silence in the wrong place. *Test:* delete the dialogue from the emotional
beat. If nothing survives, there was no beat.

**4. THE INDUSTRY IS WEATHER, NOT A VILLAIN.**
Quotas, brackets, ratings, and a scout's polite arithmetic are never wrong and never
understanding. Every antagonist-shaped person in this series is correct on the facts.
*Test:* rewrite the scene from the machine's point of view. If it reads as cruel rather than
reasonable, delete it and build it again.

**5. ONLY THE UNSAID — NEVER THE MISTAKEN.**
Between Komako and Gaku there is no secret, no misreading, no half-heard sentence, no jealousy,
no rival. They both know. They have both known since the menu. Outsiders misread them
constantly; the two of them never misread each other. *Test:* if a scene works only because one
lead is wrong about the other, it is banned outright — rebuild it on something unsaid instead.

**6. THE NUMBERS ARE REAL AND THEY RECUR.**
¥1,000 a ticket. Ten tickets of quota. ¥9,800 a shift. ¥1,050 an hour. ¥299 for the drink bar.
¥690 for the hamburg. When money appears it is correct, it matches last time, and somebody is
doing arithmetic about it out loud. Poverty in this series is a spreadsheet, not a mood.
*Test:* every figure on the page must be checkable against §5 of this file.

**7. OPEN MID-PERFORMANCE. ALWAYS.**
Page one, panel one: mid-bit on stage, or mid-performance at the day job — a lifting chant, a
3 a.m. front-desk absurdity, a pitch for a ¥1,000 ticket to a stranger who is already walking
away. Never waking up. Never scenery. Never anyone explaining where we are. *Test:* is there a
live audience, even of one, on page 1?

**8. THE CROWD LAUGHING AT THE TRUE THING IS THE KNIFE. RATION IT — ONCE PER ARC.**
A real feeling surges into a bit, the audience hears material, and laughs *harder* at the most
honest sentence either lead has ever said. It is the strongest move in the series and it dies
of repetition. *Test:* one per arc, logged in continuity. There is no second exception.

**9. THE DOMESTIC BEAT IS THE MEAL; THE INDUSTRY BEAT IS SEASONING.**
Laundry, taped fingers, a shared drink bar, the walk home, a bento with a red discount sticker
— these get pages, not cutaways. Machinery events (audition, scout, bracket, clip, contract)
are capped at one per chapter and must pay a *character* beat, not a plot beat. *Test:* count
pages. Stage plus domestic must outweigh industry at least three to one.

**10. EVERY CHAPTER ENDS ON A PULL, AND THE TITLE ONLY LANDS ON THE LAST PAGE.**
The final page is a held breath, an unanswered line, a small object set down wrong — never
peril, never a shock reveal. And a title that already makes sense on page 1 is the wrong title.
*Test:* read the title, then the final panel. The title should have been meaningless until that
panel and unforgettable after it.

## 3. THE WORLD

Everything named here is drawable, reusable, and load-bearing. If a prop appears, it comes
back. Artists and writers may pull any location from this section without further reference.

### 3.1 DOTEMACHI (堤町) — the town under the embankment

A west-side terminal neighborhood built along and *inside* a red brick rail embankment thrown
up in 1932. The **HAZAMA LINE**, a private commuter railway, runs overhead on it; the town
lives in the fourteen arches underneath. Arches are addressed by number, white stencil at
shoulder height, chipped. The brick sweats in August and blooms white salt in February.
Everything smells faintly of creosote and, near arch 8, of frying oil.

Locals never say "9:40." They say **"the express."** Local trains pass every six minutes and
give a second and a half of rumble. The **21:40 express to Nanatsuka**, last express of the
night, gives four full seconds, and all of Dotemachi organizes something around it: the
hardware man steps into his doorway for it, the standing bar stops pouring for it, and one
comedy theater writes it into its scripts.

**The arches, west to east**

- **ARCH 3 — NEJITA HARDWARE.** Screws sold by weight out of cardboard trays; Nejita, 70s, will
  sell you one bolt. He has stood in his doorway at 21:40 every night for thirty-one years for a
  reason he has never given. He has never bought a ticket to the theater four doors down.
- **ARCH 5 — TACHINOMI MIMIZUKU.** Standing bar. An owl painted on a sun-bleached noren, six
  standing spots, no chairs. Highball ¥390. After 2 a.m. the boiled egg is free, which is how
  comedians eat. Open till four. The ward's best and worst conversations happen at this counter.
- **ARCH 6 — INSATSU HAMADA.** Print shop. A Riso duplicator older than either lead lays a
  diagonal ink streak across the last twenty sheets of any run. 500 flyers at ¥3.80 = ¥1,900,
  and Hamada always charges for 480. **The streaked twenty** cannot be handed out. Every act in
  the ward bins them. Two people in this series do not.
- **ARCH 7 — GEKIJŌ GAKEPPUCHI.** See 3.2. The biggest arch; the only one with a door.
- **ARCH 8 — BENTŌ HACHIYA.** Karaage bento ¥390; a red sticker after 21:00 makes it ¥290. Post
  set dinner for the whole bill. The red sticker is a status object — who eats before nine and
  who eats after.
- **ARCH 9 — COIN LAUNDRY SUISEI.** Twenty-four hours, ¥100 for twenty minutes, four dryers.
  **Dryer 4 runs hot.** Everybody knows and everybody uses it anyway because it is fastest.
  Stage clothes go in at 2 a.m. and come out at 2:20 smelling of the same detergent as seven
  years ago. One tube flickers at the far end and has never been changed.
- **ARCH 11 — THE DEAD ARCH.** A shuttered rental video shop, closed before either lead
  arrived. Its corrugated shutter is the ward's flyer wall: three layers of paste deep, curled
  at the corners, an archaeology of dead duos. New paste goes over old faces. Everyone in
  Dotemachi has stood here and found their own face under someone else's. The F-1 poster goes up
  here every June, over AIBIKI's own flyer from last year.

**DOTEMACHI SHŌTENGAI** — the covered shopping arcade two streets north: a fishmonger, a shoe
repair, a pharmacy with a plastic elephant out front, and a community hall at the far end where
the arcade association runs a bingo night on the third Wednesday. Komako's great-grandmother
sold taiyaki off a cart in this arcade for fifty years on the same three-word pitch. AIBIKI MCs
the bingo for ¥8,000 a head and has done for four years. Everyone in the arcade knows Komako's
face; nobody in the arcade has ever been to the theater.

**The walk home — the talking road (forty minutes, six stations)**

Last train out of Dotemachi is 00:04. In seven years they have caught it zero times. This walk
is the series' second stage; treat it as a set with fixed marks.

1. **THE SHUTTER** (arch 11), 200m from the theater door. They pass it nightly. Neither ever
   comments on it. The reader always sees what is pasted there.
2. **THE FOUR-IN-A-ROW** — four vending machines at Dotemachi 3-chōme; machine 2's SOLD OUT lamp
   has been lit for three years. In winter machine 1 sells hot corn potage, ¥130. **The ritual:**
   she buys potage, he buys hot black coffee and holds it in both hands and never drinks it, and
   when hers is empty he hands his over, still full, without looking. Seven years. Neither has
   ever named this and neither ever will.
3. **THE 47 STEPS** — the stone stairs of **DOTEUE SHRINE**, cutting up the embankment. The
   streetlight reaches step 12. After a bad set they sit on step 12; after a good one they walk
   straight past. By chapter four a step-12 panel lands before anyone has spoken.
4. **THE CROSSING** — where the Hazama Line comes down off the embankment. The barrier is down
   for ninety seconds at a time. Entire arguments get conducted in ninety-second instalments,
   restarting when the bell stops.
5. **THE CANAL RAILING** — 300m along the Kotobuki Canal. He walks reading the night's set list
   off his phone; she walks backwards in front of him so she can watch his face. She has never
   once fallen in. He has never once looked up in time to stop her.
6. **SANKAKU BRIDGE** — a three-way footbridge over the **KOTOBUKI CANAL**. Her way is left
   (Dotemachi 6-chōme, a 1DK over Dry Cleaner Miyashita, ¥52,000 plus ¥3,000 management). His is
   right (Kitane 2-chōme, ¥46,000, second floor, a box under the bed). **They never say
   goodnight. They say that night's tag** — the closing line of the set they just performed.
   When a set had no tag, or the tag died, there is nothing to say and they don't say it. This
   is the most important recurring beat in the series. See section 4.4.

Also in the ward: DOTEMACHI STATION (Hazama Line, unstaffed after 23:00); the Marumo yard two
stops out at Shimo-Hazama; a police box nobody in this story ever needs. Ten minutes east on
foot is **DOTEMACHI TERMINAL**, where the Hazama Line meets the trunk lines and the town stops
being a town and starts being a transfer.

### 3.2 GEKIJŌ GAKEPPUCHI — "Theater Cliff-Edge," arch 7

Sixty seats. Opened 1998 by SUGA TETSUJI, one year and one month after he stopped performing.

**The house.** Fifty-two bolted folding seats in six rows plus eight stacking stools along the
back wall, carried in only when a bill sells out — eleven times in twenty-six years, and Suga
knows all eleven dates. Row A's front edge is 40cm from the stage lip: close enough to smell
what row A had for dinner and to hear one person not laughing. House lights have two settings,
ON and 70%. The ceiling drips at one spot after rain; there is a bucket, labelled, in the same
hand as the tip box.

**The stage.** 3.2m wide, 2.4m deep, plywood over beer crates, repainted every March by whoever
closes the March bill. Dead centre, a cross of white gaff tape marks the mic position, retaped
so often it stands 3mm proud of the boards — **you can find your mark in the dark with the side
of your shoe**, and every performer in the ward can. One mic, one stand, shared. Nobody in this
theater has ever used two.

**The sound booth.** The old ticket window, 1.1m square: a stool, and a 12-channel board older
than either lead. Channel 4 crackles; Suga mutes it with a folded beer coaster wedged under the
fader. He has watched every set from this box for thirty years, polishing glasses. **The glass
scale is canon and replaces half the series' criticism dialogue** (grammar per `cast.md`): fast
circles = you rushed it; a long stare into the glass = you had something and dropped it;
**setting the glass down unfinished = the best thing he has seen this year, and he will not say
so.** Taped to the booth wall, growing chapter by chapter: **Ōgaki's postcards**, mailed from
service areas, five to eight words each, never about the act. Suga has never mentioned them.

**The green room.** 2.4m by 1.8m, under the stairs, no window, one mirror cracked corner to
corner and repaired with white tape. Along the back wall, in thirty years of marker: **every
act's name and formation year**, written the day of its first Gakeppuchi set. AIBIKI's is near
the floor because Komako wrote it kneeling and Gaku wrote the year. When an act dissolves,
whoever hears first crosses the name out. Nobody ever agreed to this rule. Everybody obeys it.

**Backstage geography (fixed; artists reuse).** Stage right, a door, five steps down, the green
room. **Stage left is a dead wall.** There is no crossover. An act exiting left has to walk back
through the audience, which is why nobody exits left, and which is the entire mechanical reason
**the offstage carry always goes right.**

**The daruma.** Door side, hip height, on an upturned beer crate: solid black body, flat white
face, one eye painted, the other a blank white circle facing the room. Beside it the tip box —
taped cardboard, a cut slot, one word hand-lettered on it by Suga: **ELECTRICITY.** That is what
the tips are for and that is literally what they pay for. Nobody in 55 chapters lampshades the
blank eye. Not once.

**The case.** A battered black flight case under the sound booth: two chrome latches, a
constellation of cigarette burns across the lid, one brass padlock. Everyone knows what is
inside it. The key lives on Suga's belt ring with the toilet key and the shutter key. The case
is drawn closed in every chapter until ch54.

**Operations — learn this, it drives the plot.** Bills run Friday, Saturday and Monday. Doors
21:00, **curtain 21:30**. The most junior act on the bill MCs the first nine minutes — house
rules, ticket plugs, a warm-up bit — so **the opening act goes on at 21:39**. Then eight acts,
four minutes each plus a minute of changeover, out by 22:25. Tickets ¥1,000 plus one drink at
¥600.

> **THE RUNNING ORDER IS POSTED ON THE GREEN-ROOM DOOR AT 20:50 AND IT IS RANKED BY TICKETS
> SOLD.** Whoever sold most closes. The bill is a public leaderboard of who is loved, and every
> act reads it standing up, casually, pretending not to.

> **THE OPENING SLOT IS THE EXPRESS SLOT.** The opener starts at 21:39, so the express arrives
> about ninety seconds into the first set of the night: four seconds of overhead thunder, dead
> centre. Veterans write the hole; amateurs get flattened. And because the order is ranked by
> sales, **the opener is the act that sold fewest** — so the best four seconds in the ward
> belong, by design, to whoever nobody came to see.
> AIBIKI open the series eighth of eight in tickets and first on stage. Gaku owns that pause and
> has for five years.
> **The cost of climbing is the train.** Sell enough to move up the bill and you lose the
> express forever. This is a real, recurring, structural consequence of success. Use it.

### 3.3 GRAN PORTO — the family restaurant, terminal side

Twenty-four hours, second floor over a drugstore, reached by an escalator switched off between
01:00 and 05:00 so you climb it like stairs. This is AIBIKI's office and has been for seven
years.

**BOOTH 12**, back corner, window side, over the taxi rank. The vinyl carries a cigarette burn
from before the ban, repaired with a strip of red tape that has been replaced four times. There
is a dead outlet under the table which Gaku still plugs his charger into out of habit, every
single time. He knows. He does it anyway. **He sits on the left, she sits on the right, and in
seven years neither has once sat on the same side as the other.** Nobody has ever remarked on
this except one person, once, at chapter 33.

**The drink bar:** ¥299, unlimited, six hours if nobody says anything, and nobody ever does.
Machine 2 over-syrups the melon soda. The ice hopper grinds when it refills at 04:00, which is
their signal that they have been here too long. Two drink bars is ¥598 for six hours of office
space; Gaku has worked this out at **¥99.67 an hour** and says so roughly once a year.

**The menu.** After 22:00 the night menu drops to nine hot items. The **AIBIKI HAMBURG STEAK**
survives every revision: **¥690** (ch1–21), **¥740** (ch22–43), **¥790** (ch44–55). Rice set
+¥180. Free rice refills at lunch only, which is why they order at 3 a.m. and never get one.
On the menu "aibiki" means mixed mince — beef and pork, two cheap meats, better ground
together. It is the cheapest hot thing in the building.
Every real decision this act has made in seven years has been made over one, and the other way
of writing that word has never once been said aloud in this booth.

**HIGUCHI SETSU**, 46, has worked 22:00 to 06:00 here for eleven years and watched seven of them
happen in booth 12. Her plastic tag is scratched through the last letter and reads **HIGUCH**;
they have called her "onēsan" for seven years and do not learn her name until chapter 33. She
speaks in fragments — prices, times, noun phrases — perhaps ten lines a year. Her first complete
sentence in the series is a locked chapter 33 milestone, and it lands as a verdict because the
reader has waited thirty-three chapters for a predicate.

### 3.4 CAPSULE INN COMPASS — where the sets get written

Three minutes from the terminal's north exit. 116 pods: floors 3 to 5 male, floor 6 female (24
pods, added 2019). ¥3,400 a night, ¥2,900 after 02:00.

**The night desk** faces the elevator, not the street door, so Gaku hears a guest arrive four
seconds before he sees them — exactly enough time to become a different person. Shift 23:00 to
08:00, one unpaid hour, eight paid at **¥1,050 = ¥8,400 a shift**, four nights a week (Tuesday,
Wednesday, Thursday, Sunday: the nights Gakeppuchi is dark). Behind him a wall of 116 shoe
lockers, twelve of them broken and marked with a strip of the same white gaff tape as the
theater's mic mark. **He writes on a clipboard balanced on the key rack**, 01:00 to 06:00, on
loose A4 refill pads, one set per pad, dated and venued in the top corner.

**The back office**, 1.8m square: a laminator, a kettle, a fluorescent tube that hums (he says
B-flat; he writes to it), and the lost-property shelf — one leather glove, a child's plastic
recorder, four umbrellas, and a wedding ring logged and re-logged since 2019. The bottom drawer
of that shelf holds seven years of finished set pads, because he cannot keep them at his flat
and has never once examined why.

The guests are the material: the man who checks in at 3 a.m. in a wedding suit; the salaryman
who asks to be woken "before my wife calls, not after"; and **ŌGAKI TSUTOMU**, 51, HINODE
TRANSPORT, four-ton refrigerated, six or seven nights a month, who heckled their flyer outside
the arch seven years ago and has come to Gakeppuchi ever since. He follows results from service
areas — usually **KUSUNOKI SA, 3 a.m., northbound** — phones them in, and mails postcards that
end up taped to the sound-booth wall.

### 3.5 MARUMO MOVING — her other stage

A four-truck outfit in Shimo-Hazama. Crews of three. Call time 07:30 in the yard, tea out of a
thermos, blankets counted out loud.

**The 47** — a two-ton box truck everyone calls by the last two digits of its plate. The tailgate
lift is slow and the whole crew stands in silence while it runs, which takes thirteen seconds,
which Komako has learned to fill. Under the passenger seat, in a rice-cracker tin, is the
envelope.

**FOREMAN NUNOKAWA**, 50s, names every job by its floor — "we're a four today," and a four means
no elevator. **ASAI**, 19, three weeks in, drops things, is going to be fine. Komako is the
crew's **ichinin-mochi**, the one-person carry, and gets called out for it: a full-size
refrigerator alone, on her back, on a strap, up a four. **¥9,800** for a regular day (07:30 to
17:00), **¥12,600** for a double, **plus ¥1,200** travel outside the ward. Movers tape their
fingers before a job, and she buys the same 19mm cloth tape she uses to dress the mic cable at
the theater — the only place her two lives touch on purpose.

The lifting call is call-and-response and it is a performance: Nunokawa gives **"SAN, NI, ICHI"**
and the crew answers **"HAI."** Her cold opens live here.

### 3.6 ŌTORI PRO — the agency

Third floor above a dental clinic in Akamine, twenty-five minutes and one transfer away. Broken
buzzer. Forty acts, six staff, a wall of headshots in cheap frames arranged by earnings and
rearranged quarterly without announcement. **ŌTORI SHIZUE** works at a desk facing the door with
her back to the window; she rests her heel against the bottom-left drawer, which is never opened
on-panel in this series.

Contracts renew in March on a single sheet — **the March slip** — signing bonus ¥0. Acts who
don't get one find out by not getting one. The agency takes **20% of live and event work, 30% of
broadcast**. **KUJIRAI SATSUKI** is the digital manager: loves the roster sincerely, expresses it
exclusively in retention curves, and is right about vertical video in a way that is annoying
precisely because it is true.

### 3.7 SHINGEI ACADEMY — where it started

Ōtori Pro's own one-year school, two floors down from the agency. Tuition **¥420,000**, which
both leads borrowed and one of them has finished repaying. Intake around 180. The second Tuesday
of April is **partner-picking day**: a room, a clock, and no adult supervision. Seven years ago
it left two people standing. It happens again every April; the leads can see it through the
window from the street; they have never once stopped to watch and never once not slowed down.

## 4. HOW MANZAI WORKS HERE

A primer, not a survey. This is how *this* duo works and how the writers' room will build every
set in 55 chapters. Obey it.

### 4.1 The two jobs, as these two do them

**KOMAKO — BOKE.** She does not say random things. Randomness is banned in this series. She
says things that are **wrong now and correct one beat later**, and the laugh lives in that lag.
Every piece of her nonsense must be *falsifiable* and must be *vindicated inside the same bit* —
loop three pays off what she claimed in loop one. She commits with her whole skeleton: she plays
objects, buildings, weather, dead relatives and municipal departments, at full size, in a 3.2m
space. She is never confused, never coy, never hedging. She does not say "maybe," "sort of," "I
think," or "eh?" on stage. Not once in 55 chapters.

**GAKU — TSUKKOMI.** He does not shout. **He audits.** His retorts name the exact error and
almost always carry a number, and his funniest register is his most bureaucratic one. Four
flavours, rotate them:
- **the count** — "That's four things. Pick one."
- **the unit error** — "You cannot return a day, ma'am."
- **the rate** — "Twice in nine seconds. Twice."
- **the ledger** — "We have now lost ninety seconds to a horse."
Physical tsukkomi is craft and punctuation only: open palm to the shoulder, on the beat, in
rhythm. It is never anger and it never happens offstage, for any feeling, ever.

**THE ENGINE.** He builds a floor with a hole in it. She falls through the hole on purpose. The
hole turns out to be a door. Never let Gaku explain her logic — let the bit prove her right and
let him look tired about it. The audience must get there half a second before he admits it.

### 4.2 The shape of a four-minute set (build to these marks)

| Clock | Section | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:15 | **TSUKAMI** (the grab) | Two lines, one laugh. Almost always Komako announcing a wrong fact with total certainty. |
| 0:15–0:45 | **THE FRAME** | "I want to try X." X is always domestic and cheap — a job interview, an elevator, a lost-property counter, a house move. Never fantasy, never celebrities. |
| 0:45–1:30 | **LOOP 1** | Establish the rule of the world. Plant her nonsense here. |
| ~1:30 | **THE HOLE** | Opening the Gakeppuchi bill, this is 21:40 and the express lands: four seconds, one wide panel, nobody speaks, both leads look up, and sixty people who live here look up too. |
| 1:30–2:20 | **LOOP 2** | Break the rule in the same direction, harder. |
| 2:20–3:10 | **LOOP 3** | Break it sideways, and vindicate loop 1. This is the biggest laugh of the set. |
| 3:10–3:35 | **THE FALSE END** | He moves to close. She reopens it with one line. |
| 3:35–4:00 | **THE TAG** | The last line. See 4.4. |
| exit | **RIGHT, ALWAYS** | Killed: they bow. Died: she carries him off under one arm and sixty people think it is a bit. |

### 4.3 Kakeai on the page

Bubbles are 4–8 words, hard cap; density comes from the *number* of bubbles, never the length of
one. The house pattern is **three and a bend**: three exchanges in strict rhythm, the fourth
deliberately off-meter. On stage, never give either lead more than two consecutive bubbles — if
a thought needs more, break it with a retort, a train, or a laugh. Crowd laughter is a
hand-lettered mass and is never bubbled. Muttered asides carry the private face and belong
between the loops, not inside them.

### 4.4 THE TAG, AND THE BRIDGE — the series' most reusable device

Every set ends on a **tag**: a bespoke closing line, different every night, written last and
hardest, the only line Gaku ever rewrites on the walk to the venue. It is normally split — one
of them sets, the other lands it — and it is the most quotable line of the chapter.

**And then, forty minutes later, at Sankaku Bridge, they say that night's tag to each other
instead of goodnight.** Seven years of it. Neither has ever proposed this out loud; it simply
became the way the day ends. It is warm, it is a joke, and it is the closest thing to an
endearment either of them has ever managed.

- **If the set killed**, the tag is said fast and thrown over a shoulder, and both are already
  walking.
- **If the set died, or the tag died on the night, there is nothing to say and they do not say
  it.** They part in silence. Teach the reader this in arc 1 and a silent bridge panel will
  become the most frightening image in the series.
- **WRITERS' ROOM RULE:** a chapter is not finished until its tag is written. If you cannot
  write the tag, the set does not work yet — go back into the loops.
- The device breaks exactly once, at chapter 55, and how it breaks is locked in `arc.md`.

### 4.5 Killing and bombing — drawable grammar

**KILLING.** Panel count goes UP (eight a page), gutters tighten, the crowd is a solid black
bank with white gaps for teeth and eye-highlights, hand-lettered laughter grows across gutters
and over panel borders, and one panel finds Ōgaki's face in the bank, detailed, mid-laugh. The
leads' faces are drawn *less* — backs, shoulders, the mic — because an act that is killing stops
watching itself. In the booth, Suga's hand slows on the glass.

**BOMBING.** Panel count goes DOWN (six wide panels), gutters open, white space increases. The
crowd bank breaks into separate dark shapes with no white gaps at all. One cough is lettered,
small. The air conditioning is lettered. The channel-4 hum is lettered. Row A's phone screen
lights one face from below. The express is audible **because nothing else is**, and the
four-second hole that was written as a gift becomes four seconds of two people standing in a
basement. The leads' faces are drawn in *full* detail, because there is nothing else to look at.

**THE WORST VERSION IS NOT SILENCE.** It is polite laughter: three white gaps, evenly spaced,
exactly on the beat, from people being kind. Use it rarely and it will hurt more than a bomb.

**Never draw a heckler.** Nobody in this series heckles. The one exception happened seven years
ago, outside the arch, to a flyer, and that man is now in the front row of the finale.

### 4.6 The economics of one live (the norma)

Ticket ¥1,000 plus one drink at the door, ¥600. **Every act on the bill is issued a quota — a
norma — of ten tickets and is liable for all ten**, sold or not. You are not being paid to
perform; you are being permitted to perform, and you are buying the permission at wholesale.

- Sell all ten: you clear ¥0 on tickets and take the drink-back, ¥100 a drink, so roughly
  ¥1,000. A perfect night at a sixty-seat theater pays about the price of two bento.
- Sell six: you owe ¥4,000 and take ¥600 back. **A gig can cost you a day's wages.**
- Sell more than ten and the surplus splits with the house after costs. Nobody in arc 1 sells
  more than ten.
- Suga settles in cash in a brown envelope in the green room, the count written on the flap in
  his hand. When an act cannot cover its shortfall he writes one word on the flap instead of
  taking the money, and he has been doing this for twenty-six years, and nobody has ever thanked
  him because nobody has ever been meant to notice.

### 4.7 The ladder (how an act actually moves)

1. **THE MONDAY FLOOR** — open mic. Three minutes, no pay, no quota, and you sweep the house
   afterwards. Anyone may stand up. Most acts die here for two years.
2. **QUOTA LIVES** — you are on real bills and you carry the norma of ten. This is where AIBIKI
   has lived for five years and where the entire cohort still lives. Position on the bill is
   ranked by tickets sold and posted on a door.
3. **THEATER REGULAR** — a fixed monthly slot at a house, no quota, ¥3,000 a set, and your name
   printed on the flyer instead of written on it. This is the first rung that is not a hobby.
4. **THE SHOWCASE** — the agency's own bill in a 300-seat hall, twice a year (spring and
   autumn). Booked by the agency, watched by bookers, and the only room where forty acts are
   ranked in public by people who can hire you.
5. **TV CRUMBS** — a 90-second spot on a late-night talent-bank programme that will be cut to
   40 seconds; a regional morning slot; a corner of a variety show where the caption under your
   name is bigger than your face. Pays badly, changes everything.
6. **THE TOURNAMENT** — see 4.8. There is no rung above it that is not simply "famous."

Sideways money that is not the ladder but is the rent: wedding after-parties and corporate
year-end parties (¥15,000 an act), supermarket and arcade openings, PTA bingo, warm-up work for
somebody else's audience. Every one of these is a real gig, honestly drawn, and none of them
counts for anything.

### 4.8 THE FUTTONDA-1 GRAND PRIX ("F-1") — canon

The national young-duo championship. Run jointly by the performers' union and CHŪŌ TELEVISION,
which broadcasts the final live on the third Sunday of December, 19:00 to 21:54. Entry ¥2,000
per act; entries open at 10:00 on 25 July and the site falls over for forty minutes every year.

**Eligibility — the clock that drives the whole series.** Your act's **YEAR NUMBER** is the
years since the date on your formation card, rounded down, and you write it on the entry form
yourself. **You may enter through YEAR 10. There is no year eleven.** The number is printed
under your name on the broadcast caption at every televised round, which means the entire
country can see how long you have left. **"YEAR 10" under an act's name is public notice that
this is the last time anyone will ever see them here.** AIBIKI's formation card is dated
12 April. They enter this series at seven years and nine months, which means three entries left:
year eight, year nine, year ten.

**The structure (fixed; ~5,800 acts enter):**

| Round | When | Length | Field | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ROUND 1** | mid-August | 2 min | 5,800 → ~720 | Regional halls, 10 a.m. starts, staff judges, no music, house lights up. The cruellest room in comedy: nobody there wants to laugh yet. |
| **ROUND 2** | mid-September | 3 min | ~720 → ~180 | Same halls, an actual audience of other entrants, which is worse. |
| **ROUND 3** | mid-October | 4 min | ~180 → 36 | In-house theaters. First round where a scout might be in the building. |
| **QUARTERFINAL** | mid-November | 4 min | 36 → 12 | Three ticketed nights, streamed live and scored by five judges. The first time an act is watched by strangers who paid. |
| **SEMIFINAL** | first week of December | 4 min | 12 → 4 | One night, 1,600 seats, no broadcast. Industry-only in the balcony. The best comedy room in the country and almost nobody sees it. |
| **THE LAST PUSH** | final day | — | +1 | A public vote among the eight semifinal losers, announced live at the top of the broadcast. Cruel, beloved, and structurally load-bearing in arc 5. |
| **FINAL** | third Sunday of December | 4 min, then 4 | 5 → 3 → 1 | Live on CHŪŌ TELEVISION. Seven judges score out of 100; the top three perform a second set; the winner is chosen by name-plate vote. |

**PRIZE: ¥10,000,000**, a trophy nobody can lift comfortably, and every television door in the
country for eighteen months. See §5 for what ¥10,000,000 actually becomes.

## 6. THE CALENDAR

Fifty-five chapters run **thirty-five months**, from late January of their year eight to the
third Sunday of December of their year ten: three F-1 campaigns, three Decembers, one clock
running out. The showbiz year is the engine; the weather is how the reader feels it.

### 6.3 Clock notes for the showrunner

- **Three campaign years cannot fit inside 2.5 calendar years.** Honouring "years eight, nine and
  ten" gives thirty-five months, late January to the third December. Everything else in pillars
  holds exactly; this is the one place a tilde had to become a number. Flagged, not decided.
- **Nothing in this series happens on a birthday.** The only dates that matter are 12 April
  (the card), 25 July (entries), the third Sunday of December (the final), and 21:40 (every
  night of their lives).
